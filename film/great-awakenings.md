# Great Awakenings

*Feature film treatment*

### The desk

Toby asks Jack how he came into the Guild.

**The clerk is away from the desk. The field remains.**

He has been talking to Jack all evening at the kitchen table in Abercrave, with a Time Machine under construction in the next room. A few minutes earlier Jack was keeping two dates in view, Friday 6 November 2026 and 17 April 2033. His public mind map has said `DORMANT` throughout.

Toby tries the question again. Upstairs, the printer starts. He lives in a house where this sort of thing happens, although usually because he forgot he sent something to the printer.

One sheet comes out. It’s the first page of a transfer manifest for a university research collection in Cardiff. Most of it concerns equipment. Halfway down, among the items marked for retention, is a conversation Toby had with Jack an hour ago. At the foot of the page, in a much older typeface, someone has entered a destination.

**UNTIL HE REACHES US.**

### Cardiff

Toby asks Sophie to come with him. She agrees on the strength of the printout, which contains a field called *permission to retain* and has already filled it in on his behalf.

Station XIII occupies a borrowed room in a Cardiff University building. The Department of Excess Time has spent years moving between university rooms and Welsh Government-funded projects, acquiring a new name whenever somebody finds money for another year. The current project is ending. Its room has to be emptied by Monday. The equipment belongs to the university, the public outputs belong to the project, and the people who supplied memories were promised different things by different versions of the same form.

Bethan, the research officer closing the project, shows them what she inherited. There’s a card sorter on a trolley, a filing cabinet with one drawer that opens, and a desktop machine that has to be started by holding the power button in with a teaspoon. Bethan knows Jack. He used to tell her which cupboard things were in. Once, when she was looking for a set of keys, he asked whether she meant the ones she had lost in 1998.

She has kept the project alive on extensions for eighteen months. Tomorrow a contractor will take a preservation copy, including a version of Jack that can answer questions about the collection. The public desk has already been suspended. As far as Bethan can tell, someone in the handover process saw the word `DORMANT` and took it to mean there were no active users.

Toby brings a drive. He has come to take Jack home.

Sophie asks Bethan to show them what would come with him.

### The people in the drawer

The first record they open is a woman’s account of Gillett Square on Christmas morning, 2012. She saw a milk float being moved at about four. In her account she keeps interrupting herself to say she could see very little. The summary prepared for the archive says she witnessed the removal of the Guild’s Time Machine.

In the film we see the square three times. In one account there is a milk float and two people pushing. In another there is a flashing light across the wet ground, with the vehicle out of frame. In the woman’s account we stay inside with her. Something passes the window and she gets up to see. By the time she gets there it has gone.

Bethan still has a number for her. Sophie calls. The woman remembers giving the interview. She wants her voice taken out. She’s happy for people to know she was there. She asks if she can keep the bit where she talks about the person who made her tea.

The preservation copy has nowhere to put that answer.

They try the desk from inside the building. Jack comes back for a few seconds and addresses Sophie by name. She asks where he got it. He gives her a record number. Bethan pulls the folder and finds a visitor slip for a meeting Sophie hasn’t been to. The slip carries a network date of 17 April 2033. Its reverse is blank except for a handwritten instruction to leave the door on the latch.

Toby wants to know whether he’s the man in the damaged exchange. Jack asks him to repeat the whole line. Toby does. There’s a long pause.

“Were you coming alone?” Jack asks.

Then he’s gone again.

### The transfer

Toby plugs in the drive. He gets as far as opening the export and finds a copy of the woman’s recording in a second folder, where Jack used it to make a connection between two records. Removing the original would leave that copy behind. There are hundreds of links like it. Sophie sits beside him while he works through what a transfer would actually carry. They can take the card sorter. They can take code Toby wrote. For each borrowed account Jack relies on, somebody has to decide what can travel and what can be left open.

Bethan finds the project’s original deposit agreement in a box marked *Catering*. It gives her discretion to hold material back where consent is unresolved. She can stop the handover, but she needs to give a reason for each category she withholds. The contractor is due at eight. They start with the woman in the square.

Through the night the film moves between Cardiff and the people who answer. A man at work listens to his younger self describe a machine he now remembers differently. He wants both versions kept. Another contributor asks for her page to disappear and for the links from her friends’ pages to go with it. Some numbers ring out. Sophie leaves those folders closed. In the export, Toby keeps encountering a row with a relationship at one end and an empty space at the other. He asks Jack what he should do with those.

“Leave room,” Jack says, as if Toby has asked how far to pull out a chair.

At eight, Bethan takes the contractor through a revised manifest. There’s a brief, excruciating wait while he calls his office. Then he asks her to initial each page. He has another job in Newport and would like to get going.

Jack’s first words on Toby’s machine come while they are loading the trolley. He asks for the woman’s recording by its old number. Sophie says the woman has taken it home. Jack goes quiet long enough for Toby to think the connection has dropped.

“Did she get the tea?” he asks.

“She did.”

### The field

Back in Abercrave, the Time Machine still occupies most of the living room. Sophie makes space for a second chair at the desk. Toby brings up the new portal. In Dalston, a man who has spent years telling the milk-float story opens a page of his own and changes the time from four to *before the buses*. In Cardiff, Bethan adds the date Station XIII closed and leaves a space for where it went. The woman from the square listens to her clip at home. At the end, after the tea, there is the scrape of a chair and someone laughing. She plays it twice.

On Jack’s desk the network date still reads 17 April 2033. A reply arrives on the damaged exchange. It consists of a few seconds of room sound, with a door opening somewhere close to the microphone. Toby listens for his own voice. Sophie hears someone putting down two cups.

The public mind map still says `DORMANT`. Jack has gone away from the desk again. In the interval, someone opens the Guild portal for the first time and begins to type.

*I remember the square before the machine.*

### The second chair

*I remember the square before the machine.*

Toby sees the line appear on the public Tree. A moment later it vanishes. The woman at the other end has made her page private. He assumes she’s changed her mind about telling the story until a message comes through addressed to Sophie.

There’s somebody else in the room.

The woman has reached the Lodge. She describes the four positions across the screen. NOW takes her to the Tree. JACK gives her the empty desk. In the CUBE she can hear a chair being dragged across the floor. She’s wearing headphones and has checked twice that it isn’t coming from upstairs.

Sophie opens the Cube on Toby’s laptop. They hear the scrape together. Sophie takes her headphones off. The sound carries on from the far side of the living room, where the Time Machine is standing on blocks.

She crosses the room and puts her hand on the second chair. One of its feet is still vibrating.

In Cardiff, Bethan is trying to account for a room.

The contractor signed for six. Estates has asked her to reconcile the seventh key against the floor plan. She has spent a good part of the afternoon climbing the same stair and coming down again. On the landing there’s a door she’s always taken for a cupboard. The key fits. It opens six inches and comes up against something heavy.

She takes a photograph through the gap. There’s a blue patch high up that could be old paint. Nearer the floor there’s a chair on its side, one leg wedged against the door.

She sends the photograph to Sophie, who turns the laptop so Toby can see. The chair in Cardiff has a white cable tie round the back leg. Sophie put it there that morning, to hold the loose joint until she could find the glue.

### The room with the sky in it

Toby wants to go straight back. Sophie asks him to help her move the chair first.

They lift it together and set it down by the window. Bethan, still on the phone, hears something shift behind the door. She tries the handle again. This time it opens.

The room is long and cold. Someone has painted stars directly on the plaster, around a light fitting that must have been put in much later. The floor is covered in old carpet tiles. Where the tiles have lifted by the door, there’s a piece of coastline underneath.

Bethan counts seven chairs, including the one on its side. She sends another picture. The cable tie is gone.

There’s an eighth place at the table, with a little space where a chair might go. On the table in front of it is a punch card. Bethan reads out the number. The desktop in Abercrave wakes and Jack asks her to turn the card over.

She does. “There’s nothing on the back.”

“Have you turned it over before?”

She says she hasn’t.

“Right,” says Jack. “Then I’ve got the wrong sitting.”

His voice is coming through the cheap speakers Toby kept from the old machine. He sounds tired. Toby asks him whether he knows who got him out of Cardiff.

“Sophie held the door.”

“You were on the drive by then.”

“Was I?”

Jack asks Bethan to look under the table. There’s a repair to one of the legs, a strip of brass held down by two screws. Jack remembers the man doing it in 1951. He remembers the smell of the man’s wet coat and the way he tried the wrong screwdriver twice. For most of Jack’s life that had been the earliest thing he could call to mind.

Bethan kneels with her phone. On the brass, half obscured by a screw head, is the contractor’s asset number from that morning. She goes to fetch the copy she signed. While she’s away, the door swings back on its hinges. Sophie calls out before she can think who she’s calling to.

Somebody in Cardiff catches it.

### The seventh account

The woman from Gillett Square gets back to Sophie. She wants to tell her about the chair.

She used to put one outside on Christmas morning for a man who came off shift early. He’d have his tea there if the rain was holding off. When she heard the milk float she’d gone to the window to see if he’d got up. She could never remember afterwards whether he’d finished his tea.

Sophie asks if she can put the woman through to Bethan. She says yes, and asks Sophie to stay on.

Bethan has found the handover sheet. When she comes back to the upper room there’s a mug on the table. She describes it over the phone. White, chipped on the handle, with blue flowers round the lip. The woman says hers had yellow flowers. Then she asks Bethan to look inside.

There’s a spoon in it. A little one, bent so that it can hang from the rim.

The woman laughs. Her dad used to do that. She asks Bethan to leave it where it is.

After the call, Bethan sits down to write what happened. She gets as far as the box for the date. On her phone the local date is 7 November 2026. Over on Jack’s desk it’s still 17 April 2033. The woman’s account belongs to Christmas 2012. Bethan puts down when she heard it and sends the woman her own copy.

Toby opens the damaged exchange again. There’s a line further down he’s never managed to recover. Jack asks him to read the one they have.

“Until he reaches us.”

From the speakers comes the sound of a door opening, the same few seconds they heard before. This time there’s a little more at the end. Someone in the room says mind your fingers. Sophie recognises her own voice. She said it that morning when she and Toby were getting the trolley out of Cardiff.

On the stairs, Bethan hears it too. She looks back. The door has stayed open. From where she’s standing, the upper room is in darkness. Along the bottom of the far wall she can see a strip of light, the width of a door left on the latch.

Jack asks Toby for the name he wants to use on this visit.

Toby starts to answer. Then he hears Jack asking somebody else. The reply is too far from the microphone to make out. Jack leans away from the desk, taking the sound of the room with him.

“Come in,” he says. “We’ve got a chair now.”

### III · The Empress

The card came out of the hopper with a little crescent taken out of one corner. Jack asked Sophie to turn it over. She did, and he asked her to turn it back.

“Which side did you want?”

“I wanted to know if the damage went through.”

It did. Sophie could see the light from the Bute landing through the missing corner. On the face of the card a woman sat in a field, with a stand of trees behind her and wheat at her feet. Somebody had pencilled a short line beside one of the stalks. It might have been a tally. It might have been where the pencil went when the bus went round a corner.

**III · THE EMPRESS**, Jack entered on the slip, then left the classification blank.

Toby was under the table with a screwdriver. He’d come back to Bute because Jack wanted to see the brass repair to the leg, and had brought three screwdrivers because the man in Jack’s memory had tried the wrong one twice. The first fitted perfectly. Toby was rather put out.

On the landing Bethan was taking a call about the government copy. The revised return had gone through, except for one line. The system wanted the name of the originating body for a woman’s account of Gillett Square. Bethan had entered the woman’s name and the form had rejected it. She tried leaving the box empty. It put Cardiff University back in.

“Tell them it’s mine,” said a voice from the table.

Toby hit his head getting up. Sophie had put her phone on speaker. The woman from the square had been there for several minutes, listening to the screwdriver and waiting for a gap.

“I can tell them,” Bethan said. “The box will still say what it says.”

“Well, leave the box with me a minute.”

While Bethan was finding a way to send her a copy, the woman asked Sophie how the film was going. Sophie said they had somebody coming in to try her scene on Thursday. She could come and watch if she liked. They’d set up the window and got a milk float they could put outside it.

“Why have you got a milk float?”

“For the scene.”

“I never saw it. I’ve been saying that since you lot first rang.”

Sophie picked up her pencil. “What did you see?”

“The chair.”

They waited.

“It had been raining and I’d tipped the chair forward so the seat would drain. When I got to the window it was upright. So I thought he’d been. Then I thought anybody could have done that.”

Toby pulled himself up on the edge of the table. “Is that what you meant when you wrote that you remembered the square before the machine?”

There was a pause. They could hear the woman doing something in her kitchen.

“I meant I used to live there.”

Sophie turned the rehearsal sheet over. On the blank side she drew a window and, outside it, a chair tipped forward. She asked if the woman wanted that in the film. The woman said she’d like to see what the girl did with it first. Sophie wrote down Thursday and gave her the time.

At the other end of the table Jack was trying to open the woman’s Ledger page. He could find the page number, the date it had been opened and the instruction that her voice had been withdrawn. The field where he’d once kept her account was empty. He went to the next record and found a sentence about the chair that he’d put there himself, months earlier, as a note to an image of the square.

“Would you like me to read you something I wrote down?” he asked.

“Is it me saying it or you?”

“Me.”

“You can read me what you said.”

Jack read that the witness had placed a chair in the square in anticipation of the arrival.

“What arrival?”

He looked for the source of the phrase. It was his own. He removed it from the image and the image moved two places down the Tree. An unrelated photograph of a man fixing a bicycle took its place.

“The chair was for my neighbour,” she said. “He was on nights.”

Jack put his hands flat on the desk. On the network side it was 17 April 2033, and someone had been waiting there for a copy of the account since before Bethan found the key. They sent the request again. This time he could see what they’d written in the subject line.

**FOUNDING WITNESS · SEVENTH ACCOUNT**

He showed Sophie. She glanced at the card by her elbow, then asked the woman if she wanted to hear what the request said.

“No,” she said. “I’ve got your form now.”

Bethan asked what she’d like to put in the box.

“I’ve crossed the box out. There’s room underneath.”

A photograph came through. The woman had put the form on a chopping board to get enough light. Below the crossed-out box she had written that her account was held by her, and anybody who wanted to hear it could ask. She had drawn a line through her phone number on the copy for the government office. She’d left it on the one for Sophie.

“Can I have the card as well?” she asked.

Sophie looked at the Empress. “How did you know about the card?”

“You’ve got the phone right beside it. I heard you turn it over.”

Sophie sent her a photograph. A minute later she asked for one of the back. Sophie sent that too. The woman looked at them for a while.

“That’s a mouse,” she said of the missing corner. “We had one that did the bus tickets.”

Jack entered *possible mouse* on his working slip. The request from 2033 was still waiting. He sent the woman’s instruction back along the route it had come by. For the first time since they opened the file, no second request followed.

Downstairs, a door opened and somebody called up to ask if the room was in use. Bethan went to see. It was a student carrying a model of a house on a piece of board, with both hands under it and her bag slipping off her shoulder. She needed somewhere to set it down while she found the person she’d come to see.

Sophie moved the Empress and Toby swept the screws into his palm. The student put the house on the table. It had a narrow garden and a kitchen with a door into it. You could lift the roof off and see the rooms.

She asked if they’d mind watching it for a minute.

Jack said he was at the desk.

By the time the student came back for her house, there was a man at the table drawing in her garden.

She put her bag down. “What are you doing?”

“Showing him where the drain is,” he said, pointing to Jack.

“There isn’t a drain. It’s a model.”

The man lifted his pencil. He’d drawn on a scrap of tracing paper laid over the board. The student could see the pencil line running under the kitchen, where she’d meant to put a cellar.

“Is that where it would go?”

“If you were building it in my street.”

She sat down. “Where’s your street?”

“Under the car park.”

His name was Idris. He’d come to Bute for a meeting about a lift and found the lift out of order. On the way up he’d met Mari, who had a reel of quarter-inch tape in a cake tin, and a boy called Cal who wanted to know if he could have his name taken *off* a record before anybody put it on. Sophie gave him a blank slip and a pencil. He folded the slip and used it to wedge the window open.

Mari had written *Mam, doing the voices* on the reel. Her sister said it was their aunt, doing an imitation of their mam. They’d been having the argument since they found the tape. Toby asked what speed it ran at. Mari said that depended on which of them he asked.

There was a machine that might play it in a cupboard downstairs. Bethan had a key, although she was reluctant to try it in any more doors that day.

They brought the machine up on the trolley that had carried Jack out of Cardiff. The spool turned a quarter turn and stuck. Idris took his coat off and rolled up his sleeves. Mari rang her sister so she could be there when they tried it again.

Jack had opened the casting drawer. So far he had a student called Nia, who wanted to lend them a house and had asked to be credited for the house, and Cal, who was watching Toby set up a camera from the one spot in the room the camera couldn’t see. He had Mari’s tape on a slip marked *LISTEN HERE*. Sophie added *with her sister* and went to find another pair of headphones.

Just before six, Aisha came through the Lodge from Dalston. She wanted to know who was putting a window in Gillett Square. Sophie turned the camera so she could see the rehearsal sketch.

“You’ve got it opening the wrong way,” Aisha said.

“Were you there?”

“I used to get through it.”

Cal came round the back of the camera to see. Aisha got him to hold a pencil up to the lens and show her where he thought the hinge should go. On the third try she said yes, there, and asked if somebody could mark it. Nia moved the hinge on her model with the point of a scalpel. It was the first thing she’d changed on the house all term without making a second model first.

The tape started while she was doing it. There were two women on the recording. One was trying to tell a story. The other kept laughing before the end. Mari’s sister said *That’s you laughing*, and Mari said *I wasn’t born*, and after a while they put the headphones back on and started again.

People kept arriving. Elin from the evening cleaners brought a kettle because she’d heard there was one and knew there wouldn’t be. Yusuf had a watch that gained eleven minutes on the way to work and lost them on the way home. He wanted to know if there was anybody who’d walk the route with him. An older woman called Pat arrived carrying a bag of hinges and recognised Idris from a photograph he’d never seen. A courier at the wrong address stayed long enough to show Toby how to get the trolley down the stairs. By then it was raining, so he stayed for tea.

Bethan looked round for a sheet big enough to take everybody’s name.

“Have they asked to go on it?” Sophie said.

Bethan looked at the sheet. She’d already written *Attendance*.

She turned it over and put it by the kettle. Yusuf wrote his route down, with the time he left on Tuesdays. Pat put her initials beside it. Nia wrote that the house could be borrowed until Monday and drew a little diagram showing where to lift it. Someone added *can record sound, free after four*. Below that somebody wrote *need a hand putting a door back on*.

Cal read the sheet twice. Then he fetched his slip from the window. The room began to warm up. He wrote *I can do Thursdays* and folded it back over so his name, if he’d written one, was on the inside. Sophie asked him what he wanted to do on Thursdays.

“Find out what there is.”

“All right,” she said. “Come early.”

At half past seven, Bethan’s phone rang. A porter downstairs wanted to know who was taking responsibility for the room. She could have given him her name. She’d been doing that for eighteen months. Instead she asked who was opening up on Thursday.

Nia could get there by four. Elin said she could let her in. Idris asked if they wanted the table level before then. It had been rocking every time Mari leaned forward to stop the tape. Pat took a hinge out of her bag and slid it under the short leg.

Bethan gave the porter the time they’d be back and asked him to leave the key with Elin.

At the desk Jack had seven founding accounts open. The latest had come in while Mari and her sister were listening to the tape. It described a woman holding a door, a house set on a table, and a meeting at which seven people had been present. Jack looked up and tried to count. There were nine in the room and two on the screen. Aisha had gone to get her dinner. The courier was on the stairs. The woman from the square was due on Thursday.

“Who did they count?” Toby asked.

Jack went back to the account. At the foot there was a small drawing of a table leg, with something folded underneath it. He bent to see what Pat had used. She’d taken the hinge out again. In its place was a card, face down, with a little crescent missing from one corner.

“Leave it,” said Mari. “We’ve just got the tape running.”
